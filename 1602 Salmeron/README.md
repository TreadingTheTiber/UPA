*Note on this entry: Alfonso Salmerón, S.J. (1515–1585), of Toledo, the youngest of St. Ignatius's first companions and papal theologian at all three periods of Trent, is the* ***earliest Jesuit witness in the affirmative census*** *— named by Suárez, by Diana's roll-call (and Murga's copy of it), by Ripalda at n. 80, and by Mazzella; yet none of his text was in this collection until now. He writes under* ***Pius V***, *whom he names in the text, filling the per-tempora chain between Arnaldo Albertini's Julius III (1555) and Francesco Albertini's Clement VIII (1610). Disputatio II asks how the Church can be known at all, and reaches the pope-singular by way of that question. Four* præfanda *and four rationes establish that wayfarers cannot know the Church by natural evidence; then two rationes argue that* ***divine faith reaches the particular*** *— the "kingdom of Faith is too much narrowed" if faith grasps only universal dogmas, since divine faith "extends to many particular, even non-catholic truths with firm and undoubted assent." The completeness clause comes first and is unusually complete: public heretics, "if it happened that such men were raised to the Pontificate, would* ***not*** *be acknowledged as true vicars of Christ and legitimate"; and where several claim with colourable grounds, "then no one would be received as certain and undoubted Pontiff." Then the rule: "***Outside of these events, whoever is proposed in that chair as true Pope, and declared to the whole Church, is to be accepted and venerated with firm and certain faith as the undoubted and legitimate Spouse of the Church, Doctor, and Pastor***" — proposal-to-the-whole-Church and the exception-clauses stated together, decades before Bordoni and Diana. Salmerón then states the* ***opposing view as an objection*** *(printed 184, marginal "I./II./III. argum."): those "imbued with another opinion" who confine Catholic Faith to the universals ("Every man canonically entering upon the Apostolic chair is the true Pastor of the Church"), the* falsum-subesse *objection from the occult conditions of promotion, and the parity objection naming the reigning pope — "if by divine faith we are compelled to receive* ***this Pius V*** *as Pope and Pastor of the universal Church, the same judgment would have to be passed on the rest of the Church's ministers, Cardinals, Bishops." His answer opens with the line that gives this entry its edge: "***Yet I fear... lest the kingdom of Faith be narrowed by these reasons, and by that doctrine many be rendered Academics and doubters in things which were to be apprehended with firmest faith***" —* Academici *meaning skeptics. The resolution is a composition doctrine with a distinction of his own: "although* ***Catholic*** *Faith per se primo and immediately does not extend to these particular and practical things, yet if to those particulars we join something otherwise known and evident by natural evidence, we shall rightly affirm that they fall under* ***Divine Faith***. *And pointedly I said,* under Divine Faith" — because "when our Faith descends to believing these practical and singular things,* ***it is not properly Catholic, but divine and infused by God, because these particulars are not proposed to all for belief; nevertheless it is proper both to Divine and to Catholic faith that falsehood cannot underlie it***." Note what this does and does not concede: the singular is held by* ***divine, infallible faith*** *(falsehood cannot underlie it), and is denied only the name "catholic," on the ground that these particulars are not proposed to all — the mirror image of the idiom later used by Esparza and Ripalda ("de fide publica et catholica"), reached by the same composition of a revealed universal with a naturally evident particular. Nine further rationes (printed 186–190) secure the chain the objection threatened: without divine-faith certainty of ministers there is no certainty that we are in the true Church, that this council is legitimate, that these are true Christ's ministers; the sacramental reductios (baptism, absolution, the adored Host); and the* ***minister's-intention*** *argument in its earliest Jesuit form — a private intention cannot vitiate what the Church's public intention in the form secures — the objection this collection catalogues from Cajetan through Alvalate. The satellite (t. XII, on the power of General Councils) supplies the canonical-election converse in the sharpest terms in the corpus: one not canonically elected "***is rather a demon than a vicar of Christ, bearing the keys not of the kingdom of heaven but of hell***." Classification is* ***1a*** *(fides divina of the singular by composition, with falsehood excluded; the withheld predicate "catholic" is a claim about universal proposition, not a lesser assent — see the 1666 Esparza and 1652 Ripalda entries for the same doctrine under the opposite label).*

## Disputatio II — the completeness clause and the rule (printed 183)

[...] For if it happened that such men were raised to the Pontificate, they would not be acknowledged as true vicars of Christ and legitimate, since many are raised to such a rank and each lacks not grounds for defending himself: for then no one would be received as certain and undoubted Pontiff. **Outside of these events, whoever is proposed in that chair as true Pope, and declared to the whole Church, is to be accepted and venerated with firm and certain faith as the undoubted and legitimate Spouse of the Church, Doctor, and Pastor.**

## Disputatio II — the objection naming Pius V (printed 184)

**III. argum.** Lastly, if by divine faith we are compelled to receive **this Pius V** as Pope and Pastor of the universal Church, the same judgment would have to be passed on the rest of the Church's ministers, Cardinals and Bishops, since no reason appears why we should receive by divine faith rather that this man is the true Pontiff and Pastor of the Catholic Church, than that others marked out are to be received with Catholic faith as true Bishops and Priests [...]

## Disputatio II — the resolution (printed 184)

**Yet I fear, if this matter be weighed more closely and discussed, lest the kingdom of Faith be narrowed by these reasons, and by that doctrine many be rendered Academics and doubters in things which were to be apprehended with firmest faith.** [...] although Catholic Faith per se primo and immediately does not extend to these particular and practical things, **yet if to those particulars we join something otherwise known and evident by natural evidence, we shall rightly affirm that they fall under Divine Faith. And pointedly I said, under Divine Faith**: for although divine faith, infused by God, is in essence altogether the same habit as Catholic Faith [...] **yet when our Faith descends to believing these practical and singular things, it is not properly Catholic, but divine and infused by God, because these particulars are not proposed to all for belief; nevertheless it is proper both to Divine and to Catholic faith that falsehood cannot underlie it** [...]
