Those two faiths are nevertheless distinguished by their objects: because that is Catholic which apprehends Catholic dogmas; while Divine is that which believes the things that have been in some manner revealed by God, or are evidently and necessarily deduced from things revealed. For as the practical and the speculative intellect are in essence one and the same, yet are distinguished by their objects, and Intellect is the genus for both: so infused or divine faith is the genus for the Catholic and the non-Catholic, and is the same in essence, and they differ only by their objects, Catholic or non-Catholic. And because, in that whereby it assents to non-Catholic objects, it has no distinct name, therefore in this Disputation we call it by the generic name, namely of infused, or divine, faith. For who, persuaded by human faith, would cast himself into so great a peril as to dare things set above human nature — such as to remit Sins, and out of bread to effect the true Body of Christ — unless by firm and divine Faith he apprehended that he was marked with a Divine character, and endowed with so great a power? Although, therefore, Catholic faith teaches nothing about this host, whether it has been duly consecrated; about this infant, whether he has truly been reborn; and about that man, whether he is a true Priest: yet, once there is added the evident knowledge that a Priest legitimately initiated has duly consecrated this host, and intended to utter the words of the entire form; and that he willed to baptize this infant, who placed no obstacle; and that he marked another with the sacred order of the Priesthood, rightly pronouncing the form: I cannot but apprehend by divine Faith that they have obtained those effects. Just so must we judge concerning the Pope: for although Faith teaches nothing about this designated Pius V, whether he is Pope; yet, this being supposed, that he has been Canonically promoted, and by the common consent of all the electors set up as the Pastor of the Church, to be venerated, holding the chair of Peter and the place of Christ on earth, he is now matter of faith not only divine, but also Catholic. For although truths of this kind, considered by themselves alone, are such that falsehood can lie under them: yet, certain circumstances being added, which we have said can be known with evident knowledge, not only does falsehood not lie under them, but it cannot lie under them. Nor does the argument concerning the other ministers, whether Priests or Bishops, avail anything: because far other is the case of the Roman Pontiff, the head of the Catholic Church, and Vicar of Christ by an immediate title, and that of the other ministers. For as a man maimed of hand or foot, or deprived of an eye, can still subsist, but, his head cut off, can no longer be a man: so no other Bishop or Priest is so to be heard in faith by the whole Church that his decrees must of necessity be hearkened to by all: wherefore concerning them it is not necessary that we should have certain knowledge through Catholic faith that they are true and legitimate ministers. Otherwise of the Pope, whose precepts oblige the whole Church, and whose definitions in controversies of Faith are to be received reverently and firmly; nor is it open to anyone to call them into doubt, or to impugn them. But if anyone should contend that faith does not extend itself to such particulars — since, then, it is a particular that Peter or Paul is supreme Pontiff, that these or those assembling at a Council are Bishops or Priests, divine faith would not extend to this, but human opinion, or acquired faith. But one might rightly assert that, although these means are probable and particular, yet faith is added, by which we believe that God does not forsake his Church, and accordingly that this or that Council is a true one, although it depends upon a particular fact that it is legitimate: also that this Church is the true one, and has a true Pontiff — or that God supplies his defects, as if he were true. And so it must altogether be confessed that, as evidence is certain and yet depends upon sense, which can be deceived — because the higher light of reason, which is not deceived by the fallible, supplies the defect of sense — so also the light of faith depends upon sense. And because by sense we hear the doctrine and see the signs, and discern this or that Council being congregated, and these or those men being ordained and consecrated; by reason too we receive the preaching, the scripture, and the covenants of God; and from sense and reason, fallible as they are, we conceive an infallible faith — not from these except as arousing it, but resting upon the first infallible truth. |

Whether the intention of the minister is necessary to the Sacrament.} There remains one scruple to be resolved, concerning the intention of the minister in conferring the Sacrament: because, if it be lacking, the Sacrament is not completed, since it is reckoned by all as belonging to the substance of the Sacrament itself. If therefore the minister did not intend either to baptize, or to consecrate the host, or to promote a Priest, there will be no baptism, there would be no host to be adored, and no Sacrament of ordination. Again, it will be doubtful whether that Bishop, the initiator and ordainer of Priests, was himself duly and legitimately raised to the Episcopate; and again whether the Bishop who promoted him, promoted him to the Episcopate by a Catholic and legitimate rite; and by this reasoning one could proceed to infinity, and all things would have to be called into doubt; and we should all be rendered Academics and waverers. |

Reason.} Eighthly: If the intention of the minister goes to the essence of the Sacrament, how can either a Heretic, or a Jew, or a Pagan administer a valid and true Sacrament of Baptism? {marg. The impious dogma of the Novatians and Donatists concerning baptism conferred by heretics.} For very many of them utterly deny either Baptism or its effect; whereas nevertheless the doctrine of the Fathers and of the General Councils establishes the truth of baptism conferred by Heretics in the accustomed form of the Church, and the Donatists, who think otherwise, are condemned. There is not required, then, any other intention — as neither any other faith to direct the intention — besides that of the Church, which is contained in the deed itself, and in the pronounced words of the form. And it is remarkable that of old the Church, in the controversy of the Novatians and Donatists, who asserted that Baptism conferred by Heretics was null, disputed nothing concerning the due intention of the minister — whether, namely, it was present or absent. For she knew that it was not necessary; and that the Catholic Church diligently cared for this one thing, that the form wont to be observed should be exactly kept. Hence the Roman Pontiffs, being asked whether those baptized by Pagans or by Jews were to be baptized, answered: If the form had been kept — In the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost — baptism was not to be repeated; doubting nothing concerning the will or mind of the ministers. {marg. *Nicolaus I. de consecr. dist. 4. c. A quodam.*} Wherefore Pope Nicholas the First wrote back to the consultations of the Bulgarians: *You assert that many in your country have been baptized by a certain Jew — you know not whether a Christian or a Pagan — and you ask what is thereupon to be done. Infra 19.*} *(since it is not theirs, but his), ought not to be rebaptized.* Thus he — doubting nothing of the intention of the minister; for he knew that the form rightly pronounced by the minister was enough. For otherwise it would be advantageous to us, and fruitful, to repeat Baptism, that what we had not obtained by the former might be supplied through the latter: or at least care should be taken that the Baptism conferred be repeated in that form which Alexander the Third instituted to be observed whenever a Baptism previously received was uncertain or doubtful. {marg. *Alexā. III. De baptismo, & eius effectu, ca. De quibus.*} For thus he decreed: *But those of whom it is doubtful whether they have been baptized, let them be baptized with these words premised: If thou art baptized, I do not baptize thee: if thou art not yet baptized, I baptize thee in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost. For that is not seen to be repeated, which is not known to have been done.* Thus he. But since we could have no more certainty concerning this second baptism than concerning the first, baptism would have to be repeated a third, and a fourth, and a fifth time, and so on to infinity: which surely would be nothing else than to introduce an anabaptism much worse than that which the Innovators of our age have brought in: unless we take our stand on this, that it can be sufficiently certain to us that, for baptism and for the perceiving of its fruit, the form of the Church, perfectly and entirely pronounced, is enough. And what I say of Baptism, the same would have to be judged altogether of the Sacrament of Penance, and of the other Sacraments, especially of those which cannot be repeated. {marg. What is intrinsic and essential, and what extrinsic, to every Sacrament.} For the essence of the Sacrament, like any other natural thing, consists of matter and form: for the minister, and his intention, and the disposition of the one receiving the Sacrament, are outside its essence. He therefore who administers the matter and the form completes the Sacrament, provided that he do not outwardly protest that he is unwilling to administer it, or add something to vitiate the form. For he has the intention which suffices, that is, of applying that Sacrament to the receiver: since he works this not from any other source, but from purpose and intention. {marg. That he who duly administers the Sacrament, though he indicate that he confers it with feigned or malignant mind, is nevertheless truly a liar.} And though in his heart he think it is no Sacrament, or in his mind intend to confer no Sacrament: he lies, because by the deed itself he testifies that he gives a thing which is the Sacrament, however he think otherwise within, or assert that he meant to confer it in jest and not in earnest. {marg. To whom such a one may seem like.} For he who should drive a great blow into another's face, and protest with his mouth that he wished to do him no injury, would without doubt be lying, and the deed would have to be stood by more than the word: because, as the Jurists speak, it would be a protestation against the deed. {marg. To what other he is to be compared.} So too, if a Notary should draw up an instrument with all the requisite conditions and circumstances, and yet in his heart intended in mockery to perfect such an instrument, the instrument would nevertheless in truth be valid; unless he vitiated his intention by some express word, which he set down in the instrument. So he who confects the Sacrament, and administers the matter, and applies the form — even if he afterwards assert that he meant to confer nothing, or acted in mockery — would not be to be heard. {marg. *Alex. Patriarch. Alexandr.*} And thus rightly did Alexander, Patriarch of Alexandria, counsel having first been taken with the Bishops, define that those whom Athanasius — who in his boyhood had baptized other boys playing with him on the seashore, himself acting the person of a Bishop — had baptized, were truly baptized, and on no account to be rebaptized: because he understood that the words wont to be uttered in Baptism had been rightly pronounced by him. IX. reason.} Ninthly, an argument can be taken from the intention of the one receiving the Sacrament. For though a man come to the Sacrament with a feigned or an heretical mind, believing no effect of the Sacrament: yet if he freely submit himself to the custom of the Church, and suffer himself to be baptized after the manner of the Church — as did Simon Magus, and those boys playing beside the sea who were baptized by Athanasius — they do indeed receive the Sacrament, that is, the character, though not the grace: nor is it enough to say: I did not intend to receive the Sacrament, or the character, since I did not believe, but did it in mockery. For it will be answered him: Why then didst thou suffer to be done in thee that which the Church is wont to do with all — from which that effect proceeds, and which they proclaim will come to pass, with whatsoever mind men come to this Sacrament? And so the Sacrament is not repeated in these men, even when they confess their own feigning; but, the feigning withdrawing and faith coming on, the baptism conferred now works its effect of grace in them. So likewise of one coming to Baptism truly and with Faith: even by a minister who does not believe, or who works in mockery, true Baptism is conferred, and its effect — though he sin most grievously in the ministry thereof: wherein he harms himself only, and not the other. |
