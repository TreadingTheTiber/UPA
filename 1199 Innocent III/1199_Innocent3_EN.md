# Sermones de diversis: Sermo III in consecratione pontificis (*Sermons on Various Occasions: Sermon III on the Consecration of the Pontiff*)

**Innocentius III (Pope Innocent III), preached 1199; Patrologia Latina 217, Paris: Migne**

*English translation of the central section of the sermon (PL 217, coll. 663–666), with its heading from col. 659; transcribed from the page images of Migne's edition and translated 1:1. Migne's column numbers are retained in the text; his evident misprints are flagged in square brackets.*

### [Sermon III, On the Consecration of the Pontiff — PL 217, coll. 663–666]

**SERMON III. ON THE CONSECRATION OF THE PONTIFF.** *On the four kinds of espousals, and the praises of the Roman Church; on the spiritual marriage of a bishop with his Church, and the goods of marriage.* — *He that has the bride is the bridegroom. But the friend of the bridegroom, who stands and hears him, rejoices with joy because of the bridegroom's voice* (John 3). [...]

**[col. 663]** [...] The carnal marriage, which is between man and woman, is commonly said to be initiated, ratified, and consummated: initiated in the betrothal, ratified in the consent, consummated in the union. So too the spiritual marriage, which is between a bishop and his Church, is said to be initiated in the election, ratified in the confirmation, consummated in the consecration. But that marriage which I, the Bridegroom, contracted with this my bride was at once initiated and ratified: for the Roman pontiff, when he is elected, is confirmed, and when he is confirmed, is elected. Do you not recall what you have read of him in the canon? — that the one elected, as true pope, obtains the authority of ruling the Roman Church and of disposing of all her resources. Certainly, when I was contracting, a son was taking his mother to wife; but once I had contracted, a father had his daughter as wife. In carnal marriage kin are excluded and outsiders admitted; but in spiritual marriage, at first sight and as a rule, outsiders are excluded and kin admitted. Concerning the exclusion of kin in carnal marriage, you have read it provided in the canon: "We forbid all who are near by affinity to approach the conjugal union." Concerning the exclusion of outsiders from spiritual marriage, canonical authority hands down that clerics are to have the power of resisting [the print has *remittendi*; the canon reads *renitendi*] if they see themselves overburdened, and are not to fear to refuse those who happen to be thrust upon them from outside. Hence it is found provided in the canon that at the summit of the apostolate no one be consecrated [unless] from the cardinal [the print has *carnalibus*] priests or deacons.

You therefore celebrate with me today, for the first time, the anniversary of the day on which this spiritual marriage was consummated — although I was consecrated in the Apostolic See on the very day on which blessed Peter the Apostle was established in the episcopal chair. But as the light of the sun does not allow the light of a star to be seen together with it, so that solemnity does not suffer this one to be celebrated together with it. The lesser therefore yields to the greater, because the lesser follows after the greater. I, then, contracted the marriage; by the consecration I celebrated the nuptials.

Now there are principally three goods of marriage: fidelity, offspring, and sacrament. Fidelity refers to chastity, offspring to fruitfulness, sacrament to stability. For the Roman pontiff and the Roman Church have always kept such fidelity to one another that what the Truth says in the Gospel may fittingly be applied to them: "I know my sheep, and mine know me" (John 10): they do not follow a stranger but flee, because they do not know the voice of strangers. Now the strangers are heretics and schismatics, whom the Roman Church does not follow, but pursues and puts to flight. But their own they know and hear — not an apostate, but an apostolic one; not a Cathar, but a catholic one — [she] receiving and rendering the conjugal debt: receiving from him the debt of providence, and rendering the debt of reverence. For "the husband has not power over his own body, but the wife; likewise the wife has not power over her own body, but the husband" (1 Cor. **[col. 664]** 7).

Further, since the Roman Church pays the debt of reverence to no one at all except the Roman pontiff, who after God has no other superior: how is it that the Roman pontiff is bound to render the debt of providence not only to the Roman Church, but to all the Churches altogether? "To the wise and to the unwise I am a debtor" (Rom. 1), says the Apostle; and: "My daily pressing care, the solicitude for all the Churches" (2 Cor. 11). Is it to be judged a like case, that, as is read in the Old Testament, one man could have several [wives]? Have you not read that Abraham had Sarah to wife, who nevertheless brought in her handmaid Hagar to him; nor did he on that account commit adultery, but fulfilled a duty (Gen. 16). So too the Roman pontiff has the Roman Church as his bride, who nevertheless brings in to him the Churches subject to her, that they may receive from him the debt of providence: for the more is rendered, the more is owed. But now that is done in the spirit which then was done in the flesh; for "it is the Spirit that gives life, the flesh profits nothing" (John 6). But cannot one bishopric have two bishops, and one bishop have two bishoprics? Not to seek examples far off: one and the same man is bishop of Ostia and of Velletri [the print has *Vellucensis*, with Migne's conjecture *Vercellensis*], so that both Churches at once are wedded to the same man. Again, the Church of Hippo, which was joined to Valerius, while he was still living was wedded also to blessed Augustine, who did not so much succeed Valerius as accede to him. But by what reasoning these things can be done with the law of marriage preserved, do you inquire, whom the investigation of questions delights: another care keeps me occupied.

This marriage between bishop and Church is contracted [the print has *Contra hoc conjugium*] that it may bear religious offspring to Christ, so that his wife may be "as a fruitful vine [the print has *viris*] on the sides of his house. His children as olive plants," etc. (Ps. 127). For which reason the Apostle says: "My little children, of whom I am in labour again, until Christ be formed in you" (Gal. 4). And Leah, having once given the mandrakes, "hired Jacob, that he should go in unto her, and she conceived and bore" (Gen. 30). And those whom Christ regenerates he instructs with saving doctrines and forms with honourable admonitions; he "feeds them with the bread of life and understanding, and gives them the water of saving wisdom to drink" (Ecclus. 15). — "Come," he says, "eat my bread, and drink the wine which I have mingled for you" (Prov. 9): the heavenly bread and the chalice of salvation, of which if any man taste he shall live for ever, "having in it all that is delicious, and the sweetness of every taste" (Wis. 16 [the print has *Sap. XXI*]).

But the sacrament between the Roman pontiff and the Roman Church endures so firm and stable that they are never separated from one another except by death; because, the husband being dead, the woman, according to the Apostle, "is loosed from the law of her husband" (Rom. 7). But this husband, bound to a wife, does not seek to be loosed, does not withdraw [*non cedit*], is not deposed; for "to his own lord he stands or falls" (Rom. 14). — "But he that judges is the Lord" (1 Cor. 4). Yet for **[col. 665]** the cause of fornication the Roman Church could dismiss the Roman pontiff. I do not mean carnal fornication, but spiritual — because the marriage is not carnal but spiritual — that is, on account of the error of unbelief; for "he that does not believe is already judged" (John 3): and in this article is understood what is read in the Gospel which you have heard: "You are the salt of the earth; but if the salt lose its savour, wherewith shall it be salted?" (Matt. 5). I, however, would not readily believe that God would permit the Roman pontiff to err against the faith, for whom he prayed in a spiritual manner [*spiritualiter*] in Peter: "I," he says, "have prayed for thee, Peter," etc. (Luke 22). Therefore *he that has the bride is the bridegroom.*

Now this bride did not wed empty-handed, but bestowed on me a dowry precious beyond price: namely the fullness of spiritual things and the breadth of temporal things, the greatness and the multitude of both. For the others were called to a share of the solicitude, but Peter alone was taken up into the fullness of power. As a sign of spiritual things she conferred on me the mitre; as a sign of temporal things she gave me the crown: the mitre for the priesthood, the crown for the kingdom — constituting me the vicar of him who has written on his garment and on his thigh "King of kings and Lord of lords" (Apoc. 19), "a priest for ever according to the order of Melchisedech" (Ps. 109). She bestowed on me an ample dowry; but whether I have made her any gift on account of the nuptials, you shall see. I do not wish to assert it boastfully. She sought one who knew nothing of it; she took one who resisted — gainsaying at the beginning, but consenting at the end; because consent alone between legitimate persons makes a marriage.

Hence it seems to come about — though it may seem strange — that someone can be [pontiff] of a Church before he is its bridegroom, **[col. 666]** just as someone could be the bridegroom of a Church before he was its pontiff. For when, by the provision of a superior, a bishop is justly given to those unjustly resisting [the print has *retinentibus*], before they consent to him, he is assuredly their pontiff by the authority of the grant. But it seems perhaps that he is not yet their bridegroom, for want of consent — to which they are bound to consent [the print has *teneatur*], and to enter the conjugal covenant with him. But when he is received by election, by reason of the mutual consent of the electors and the elected he is assuredly made their bridegroom, especially when the election is confirmed. Yet before he is consecrated he will claim neither the name of pontiff nor the office. But whether this is so, let our care inquire. [...]
