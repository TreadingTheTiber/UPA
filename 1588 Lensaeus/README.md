*Note on this entry: Jean Lens (d. 1593) of Bailleul, Regius Professor at Louvain in the Baius years, wrote his six books against "the various heresies and errors of this age" — a controversial ecclesiology, not a scholastic* de fide *treatise. He appears in* ***no census roll-call*** *of the disputed question, and this entry is not a positional source upon the guarantee: it is classified* ***W***. *Two things earn him a place all the same. The first is that his key passage is,* ***almost word for word, Alfonso de Castro's*** *(the 1534 entry, the negative side's textual anchor). Castro: "*Quàm uis enim credere teneamur ex fide, verum Petri successorem esse supremum totius ecclesiæ pastorem, non tamen tenemur eadem fide credere Leonem aut Clementem esse verum Petri successorem… Quò fit, vt qui obedientiam denegaret Clementi aut Leoni hac ratione, scilicet quod dicet eum non fuisse rectè electum, etsi scismaticus dicendus sit, non tamen hæreticus censendus.*" Lensaeus: "*Tametsi enim fide credere teneamur legitimum Petri successorem supremum esse totius Ecclesiæ in terris caput,* ***non tamen eadem fide recipere tenemur Leonem aut Clementem vel Gregorium verum esse Petri successorem***: *neque enim ad fidem catholicam pertinet quàm quisque Pontifex rectè aut canonicè eligatur aut electus sit: quo fit vt qui obedientiam Clementi aut Leoni denegaret, hoc solum prætextu quòd eum diceret non canonicè fuisse electum,* ***quamuis schismaticus dicendus esset, non tamen hæreticus censeri deberet***." *The same Sigismund-and-Constance frame, the same "who would dare charge with heresy any of those who called themselves pontiffs, or those who obeyed them," the same "Leo or Clement," the same schismatic-not-heretic verdict. Lensaeus adds "or Gregory," and makes explicit the distinction Castro leaves implicit: "***error in things which are of fact is one thing, error in things which are of right and of doctrine another***." He nowhere names Castro at this locus. The entry therefore documents the* ***transmission*** *of the negative side's anchor-sentence into Louvain controversial theology two generations later — the formula circulating as a commonplace, independently of the Spanish scholastics who cite Castro by name. The second reason is what surrounds it. Lensaeus is* ***institutionally maximal and personally minimal***, *and the combination is instructive. He holds that "Ecclesia" in "Dic Ecclesiæ" means the prelates, "as it were the senate and magistracy of the Church"; that a defect in defining would be a defect* ***ratione partis***, *of that magistracy; and that it cannot occur, "***because in truth, if she were to fail by error in defining, in those who preside, easily and by a ready path the error would gradually spread into the whole body of the Church***" (B) — the very premise the affirmative side builds upon. His Liber VI cap. XVIII is titled "***That the authority of the Church, which is to be looked for chiefly in her pastors, can be undermined by no errors handed down against the faith***," and there the consent of the faithful appears — "peoples so many, spread far and wide through all nations,* ***hear alike, approve with one consent, and carry out conformably***" — but running* ***downward***: *"this they owe to the concord of their pastors" (C). He grants the successor of Peter "***a certain supreme authority of judging concerning absolutely any question of Christian dogma***," exercisable* ***outside a general council*** *(the Pelagian heresy condemned by Innocent and then Zosimus with the African councils' letters, "no general council having been convened on that account"); holds that evil pastors "***do not cease to be pastors***" so long as they feed the flock with saving doctrine; and denies that judgment of doctrine belongs to all the faithful, since the critics "tear the office of judging doctrine away from the pastorate" — "***by office and charge and their proper function not all are judges of doctrine***" (D). So he affirms two of the three effects the genus names — no error imposable on the Church, and the obligation of obedience and communion (its refusal being schism) — but grounds them in the* ***office***, *never in the acceptance; the word* acceptance *never occurs in that role anywhere in the book. That is why he is neither School 1 (he denies the singular is de fide), nor School 2 (he never entertains a claimant who is accepted yet possibly not pope, and so never reaches the supply-mechanism), nor School 3 (he has no reception-guarantee, his consent runs top-down, and he expressly withholds doctrinal judgment from the faithful — the direct contradictory of the 3b structure). Above all,* ***the case he treats is divided obedience, not peaceful acceptance***: *three claimants and the forty-year schism ended at Constance — precisely the case the doctrine's own defenders exempt by the completeness clause (Diana's* pacifica; *Arriaga's "not a few malcontents but a notable, probable contradiction"; Esparza's "whole or nearly the whole"; Córdoba's own "then no one is to be received as certain and undoubted Pontiff"). His denial is therefore not a denial of the doctrine but a statement about the one scenario its defenders set aside — which is exactly the caution the 1534 Castro entry already records of Castro: the case of one claimant peacefully accepted by the whole Church "is never posed," and acceptance is never discussed "as a sign or cause of legitimacy at all." Classification* ***W***: *a witness who attests the criteria, practice and idiom the later doctrine systematizes — the schism/heresy boundary, the fact/right distinction, indefectibility located in the defining magistracy, consent as a mark — without ever formulating, affirming, or denying the guarantee itself.*

## A — Liber I, cap. XII: the Great Schism, and schism not heresy (printed 43)

[…] So also on other occasions it has come about that a schism should arise for this reason alone, that the Pontiff who was held to be such was thought not to have been rightly and legitimately elected. Such a schism is recorded to have lasted about forty years, when three men bore themselves as Pontiffs, which was at length ended in the Council of Constance, Sigismund being king of the Romans. Many, therefore, were on this account schismatics, and yet not heretics. **For who would dare to fasten the crime of heresy upon any of those who called themselves pontiffs, or upon those who obeyed them — a charge which the general Council laid against none of them? For although we are bound to believe by faith that the legitimate successor of Peter is the supreme head of the whole Church on earth, we are nevertheless not bound by the same faith to receive Leo or Clement or Gregory as being the true successor of Peter: for it does not pertain to Catholic faith how any Pontiff is rightly or canonically elected, or has been elected: whence it comes about that one who should deny obedience to Clement or Leo, on this pretext alone, that he says he was not canonically elected — although he must be called a schismatic, nevertheless he ought not to be reckoned a heretic. For error in things which are of fact is one thing, error in things which are of right and of doctrine another.**

## B — Liber III: the defect would be *ratione partis*, and cannot occur (printed 187–188)

[…] when the Church is named, the prelates themselves — as it were the senate and magistracy of the Church — are understood [...] In like manner, **if the Church were said to have erred and failed in defining anything, this failure would have to be understood as having happened in respect of a part, that is, of the ecclesiastical magistracy**: for it is not everyone's task to define concerning those things which are of faith, but the office of those who preside over the Church [...] **Because in truth, if she were to fail by error in defining, in those who preside, easily and by a ready path the error would gradually spread into the whole body of the Church.**

## C — Liber VI, cap. XVIII: the consent of the peoples owed to the pastors' concord (printed 421–422)

***That the authority of the Church, which is to be looked for chiefly in her pastors, can be undermined by no errors handed down against the faith.* CHAPTER XVIII.**

[…] nevertheless **that peoples so many, spread far and wide through all nations, hear alike, approve with one consent, and carry out conformably — this they owe to the concord of their pastors**, which they carefully and constantly preserve in handing down saving doctrine and the institutes of Christian discipline. [...]

## D — Liber VI: supreme judgment, bad pastors, and who may judge doctrine (printed 453–455)

[…] pertain to this: that we may understand **that there resides in the successor of Peter a certain supreme authority of judging concerning absolutely any question of Christian dogma** [...] (For it is established that the Pelagian heresy with its authors was convicted and condemned by the Bishops of the Roman Church, first Innocent, then Zosimus, with the letters of the African councils cooperating, **no general council having been convened on that account**.) [...] **For though some be evil, nevertheless so long as they feed with saving doctrine the flocks entrusted to them, they do not cease to be pastors.** [...] For as to what they add, *that judgment concerning doctrine pertains to all the spiritual sons of God* [...] in this they fall into the error of **tearing the office and charge of judging concerning doctrine away from the pastorate** [...] so that **by office and charge and their proper function not all are judges of doctrine**.
