# Il trionfo della Santa Sede e della Chiesa contro gli assalti dei novatori combattuti e respinti colle stesse loro armi — Discorso preliminare, §§ XXXIV, LII, LIV, LVI, LIX (*The Triumph of the Holy See and of the Church against the Assaults of the Innovators, Fought and Repelled with Their Own Weapons*)

**D. Mauro Cappellari, monaco camaldolese (afterwards Pope Gregory XVI); first published Rome, 1799; Venezia: Giuseppe Battaggia, 1832**

*English translation of five passages of the Preliminary Discourse (§§ XXXIV, LII, LIV, LVI, LIX), transcribed from the page images of the Venice 1832 edition and translated 1:1. Page numbers of that edition are retained in the text; the author's footnotes follow their sections.*

### [Preliminary Discourse, § XXXIV — pp. 56–57]

[...] In the times of the antipopes, as also when a pope has died, the form of government ordained by Christ is not obscured; for both in the case where there is a well-founded doubt, so that it is not well known who ought to be venerated as pope, and in the case of a vacant see, there happens in the Church what happens in various **[p. 57]** monarchies, in which during an interregnum the government resides in some senate — as was the practice also in the ancient Roman empire, in which the Roman senate commanded in time of interregnum; so that in those cases the government of the Church is, for the time being, aristocratic. But who does not know that this cannot be her natural state? Who can fail to recognize it from the very urgency with which the Church sets about electing her head, ill enduring to remain headless for a long time? [...]

### [Preliminary Discourse, § LII — pp. 89–90]

I know it is said that the Council admitted the acts of Gregory solely for love of peace, as for the same purpose it also admitted the solemn renunciation of Egidio Muñoz [*Munione*], canon of Barcelona, elected by only three cardinals under the name of Clement VIII, universally derided by all in his vain pretensions, and therefore a mere phantom of a pope: arguing from this that, just as the Council of Constance could have exercised a true jurisdiction over Clement VIII — recognized by all, without the least hesitation, as pontiff in name only — and yet accepted his renunciation, so the fathers likewise admitted, solely for love of peace, all that Gregory said and did, never because they doubted, or because others could doubt, that their conciliar decrees were legitimate and their definitions authoritative. But the parity can never hold, unless there be produced a declaration of the Council that it received the renunciation of Clement VIII *quia abundans ad certitudinem cautela nemini nocet, sed omnibus prodest* [because abundant caution for the sake of certainty harms no one, but profits all], as was made for the other. **[p. 90]** This is the reason alleged by the Fathers for not having opposed Gregory's proceeding; and it is precisely on this that our argument rests, since they omit it where Clement VIII is concerned. Thanks meanwhile to the objectors, who propose to us a difficulty that becomes the most victorious proof of our thesis. For to what end do they adduce it? To convince us that a like indulgence was practised by the Council both with a pope who, like Gregory, was at least doubtful, and with one who, like Clement VIII, was evidently a false pope. They recognize, then, that the spontaneous cession of the latter could be admitted without danger of misunderstanding: and so the Council admitted it without giving a reason. Not so with Gregory: and so the Council declares that it does this for greater certainty and caution, which there is no need to practise except where the object is doubtful or open to question. And this object could be no other than either the legitimacy of the Council's acts, despite the want of papal convocation and approval, or the authority to deprive of the throne a Pope even merely doubtful, in the case that he sat on it legitimately. The Council therefore resolved all these doubts by admitting what Gregory did. If this was not the mind of the fathers in those expressions, let it be said what other is to be admitted — provided there be no recourse to cavils born of a prejudiced imagination.

### [Preliminary Discourse, § LIV — pp. 92–95]

Be it so of Gregory, be it so of John; but there still remains the great obstacle of the deposition **[p. 93]** of Benedict XIII, who would never either renounce or subscribe the sentence of his deposition, so that he died in schism. Therefore, the adversaries conclude, whatever be the case with the other two, there always stands one definitive fact, which assures us of the absolute firmness the fathers had in the doctrine of their own supreme power, and binds the *papists* in such a way that they cannot get loose on any side — the deposition of Benedict, that is, of a *dissenting* pope, being universally recognized as legitimate. Are the *papists*, then, conquered, are they beaten? A fool who believes it! Rather the way is opened to them for new victories. How? Here it is. Benedict's Church was confined within the limits of *Peñíscola* alone, his adherents having withdrawn from his obedience, and among them the very cardinals who had elected him pontiff. The Council therefore had every ground for judging that his own supporters had recognized the illegitimacy and nullity of his assumption to the Apostolic See; nor, on the other hand, ought it to cooperate in the continuation of the schism by leaving him in peaceful possession of his pretended pontificate. In this hypothesis, therefore, it had every right — I will say more, the obligation — to provide for the security of the whole Church by deposing Benedict, without its being possible to infer from this that it had an equal right to depose an evidently legitimate Pope. For it pronounced and executed its final sentence not on the strength of its authority over the Pope, but on the well-founded **[p. 94]** supposition that he was not one: in which case the power of the Church is as evidently certain as it is evidently certain that Jesus Christ, willing the government founded by himself for the security of the faithful to be *immutable, visible,* and *perpetual*, must have provided the Church with all those means which are necessary in order not to let herself be governed by an illegitimate head. Hence he must infallibly have conferred on her the right, in uncertainty and in reasonable and well-founded doubt of the legitimacy of a Pope, to proceed to the election of another. And this above all if he whose legitimacy is reasonably suspect should not cease to harass her in a thousand ways; so that God himself would have to be accused of not having sufficiently provided for her indefectibility, if in such circumstances he had not furnished her with the appropriate faculties. Now what harassments did the Church not receive from Benedict, who by his deeds pertinaciously impugned the article *unam, sanctam*? He hurled the most terrible anathemas against the Council and against the adherents of the other Pontiffs, and attempted every most reckless outrage in order to keep himself on the throne illegitimately occupied: pretending that the Church of Jesus Christ, perished in all the other parts of the world, was to be found confined to Peñíscola alone, as he answered the legates of the Council: *Ibi non est Ecclesia, sed in Paniscola est vera, inquam, Ecclesia, ... hic est arca Noe* [The Church is not there, but in Peñíscola, I say, is the true Church… here is Noah's ark] (1). Whence it was possible, as **[p. 95]** Ballerini observes, to consider him a public schismatic and heretic, and consequently fallen *of himself* from the pontificate, even if he had been validly raised to it. For which reason the Council of Pisa, having already declared him *in causa schismatis et fidei contumacem* [contumacious in a cause of schism and of faith], did not even before pronouncing its sentence account him worthy of the title of pontiff, nor of the name of *Benedict* which he had assumed, but always called him by his own name, *Peter de Luna*. And if anyone should object that this our answer conflicts with the privilege of papal infallibility which we defend in the following treatise, it will help to have him reflect that the Pope is held infallible only in his solemn dogmatic definitions, never in his private opinions — much less when the whole universe is witness that in him it is not reason that speaks, but passion and interest, to the point of making him evidently appear frenzied and delirious, as happened in Benedict.

(1) *Rainal. t. 16. Conc. col. 1041.* [Raynaldus, vol. 16; Councils, col. 1041.]

### [Preliminary Discourse, § LVI — pp. 97–98]

The argument with which Tamburini thinks to bind us indissolubly therefore necessarily falls of itself. "Did not Martin V," he says, "hold himself legitimately elected in that Council (of Constance), although there were then three popes living, Benedict, Gregory and John? Therefore he held those popes to have been legitimately deposed by the Council, and the Holy See legitimately declared vacant by that Council. Therefore he approved as legitimate all the acts of that Council with respect to his election. But how could he hold those acts legitimate, if the Council had not had the authority to perform them? And how would it have had the authority necessary for this, if what the Council established to this effect in sessions IV and V were not true — namely, that the Council is superior to the Pope, and that the papal authority must obey the Council? Therefore Martin V approved in deed also the decrees of sessions IV and V" (1). The argument, I say, falls of itself; for it has been shown that **[p. 98]** the Council did not exercise a sovereign power against the said pontiffs, since the first two gave up the papal dignity of themselves, and the other, whose election could be more reasonably suspected, was of himself subject to the Council, as a doubtful pope, or even as already no longer pope: therefore Martin V could hold himself a true legitimate pope without recognizing the vaunted conciliar supremacy, which was not left defined even in the two famous sessions, understood in relation to the very facts of that Council.

(1) *Rifless. sopra un Sermone del Bossuet, pag. 50, 51.* [Tamburini, Reflections on a Sermon of Bossuet, pp. 50, 51.]

### [Preliminary Discourse, § LIX — p. 102]

What is this case? That of a doubtful pope. In this way the acts of the Council of Constance are reconciled with one another: acts in which it shows itself indeed uncertain and wavering in the recognition of the fact — whether, that is, those pontiffs, Gregory especially, could prudently be considered *doubtful* — but in nothing contradicts its authority over them, supposing they had been prudently recognized as such. Whereas on the hypothesis of *absolute* conciliar supremacy it would, in some of those same acts, openly contradict the absolute authority of Councils over all popes. [...]
