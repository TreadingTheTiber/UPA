*Note on this entry: Mauro Cappellari, a Camaldolese monk then living in Rome, published the* Trionfo *in 1799 against Pietro Tamburini and the theology of the Synod of Pistoia; he was elected pope as Gregory XVI in 1831, and the book — the only one he wrote — was reprinted at Venice the next year, the edition used here. He enters this collection as a* ***witness (W)***, *and for one thing above all:* ***he is a witness against the hypothesis of a hidden true pope — a man who would remain pope while the whole Church, no longer following him, adheres to another.*** *The passages come from the Preliminary Discourse, where Cappellari must answer the Gallican and Jansenist appeal to Constance: the Council deposed popes, therefore councils are above popes. His reply runs case by case, and its hinge is Benedict XIII (§ LIV), the one claimant who neither resigned nor consented. Why could the Council treat him as no pope? Not by authority over a pope, but because* ***"Benedict's Church was confined within the limits of Peñíscola alone, his adherents having withdrawn from his obedience, and among them the very cardinals who had elected him pontiff. The Council therefore had every ground for judging that his own supporters had recognized the illegitimacy and nullity of his assumption to the Apostolic See."*** *The collapse of the following is not treated as the misfortune of a true pope in eclipse; it is the evidence that the claim was null. And Benedict's own defence — that the Church of Christ had perished everywhere else and survived in Peñíscola alone, "here is Noah's ark" — is precisely what makes him, with Ballerini, "a public schismatic and heretic, and consequently fallen of himself from the pontificate, even if he had been validly raised to it." The remnant-theory is not a refuge for the claimant; it is his condemnation. Benedict's successor fares worse (§ LII): Clement VIII, "universally derided by all… a mere phantom of a pope… recognized by all as pontiff in name only," is for that reason "evidently a false pope." Around these stand the positive principle (§ LIV): Christ "must have provided the Church with all those means which are necessary in order not to let herself be governed by an illegitimate head"; the interregnum-doctrine for a doubtful papacy (§ XXXIV); the limitation of the Constance decrees to "the case of a doubtful pope" (§ LIX); and the outcome (§ LVI): the first two having ceded, and the third being "a doubtful pope, or even already no longer pope," Martin V "could hold himself a true legitimate pope."* *Four cautions fix the classification. (1) Cappellari never poses the school question — whether the Church's peaceful acceptance certifies the man — and formulates no guarantee; his subject is the pope's superiority to a council. (2) In this book "acceptance" (*accettazione*) almost always means something else: the Jansenist thesis that a council is known to be legitimate only by the subsequent acceptance of the universal Church, which he attacks (§§ LVII–LVIII, not printed here). He is no friend of acceptance as a constitutive condition, which makes his reasoning from the loss of obedience the more notable. (3) His case is a divided obedience in schism, not one claimant peacefully received by all; what he shows is how a papal monarchist reasons about the claimant the Church has left. (4) He attributes the reception of Clement VIII's resignation to Constance; the abdication in fact came in 1429, after the Council had closed. The sentence beginning "The Council therefore had every ground…" is often quoted without the sentence before it; both are given here, with the whole of § LIV.*

## § LIV — Benedict XIII: a Church confined to Peñíscola (pp. 92–95)

[...] **Benedict's Church was confined within the limits of *Peñíscola* alone, his adherents having withdrawn from his obedience, and among them the very cardinals who had elected him pontiff. The Council therefore had every ground for judging that his own supporters had recognized the illegitimacy and nullity of his assumption to the Apostolic See**; nor, on the other hand, ought it to cooperate in the continuation of the schism by leaving him in peaceful possession of his pretended pontificate. In this hypothesis, therefore, it had every right — I will say more, the obligation — to provide for the security of the whole Church by deposing Benedict, without its being possible to infer from this that it had an equal right to depose an evidently legitimate Pope. For it pronounced and executed its final sentence **not on the strength of its authority over the Pope, but on the well-founded supposition that he was not one**: in which case the power of the Church is as evidently certain as it is evidently certain that **Jesus Christ, willing the government founded by himself for the security of the faithful to be *immutable, visible,* and *perpetual*, must have provided the Church with all those means which are necessary in order not to let herself be governed by an illegitimate head.** Hence he must infallibly have conferred on her the right, in uncertainty and in reasonable and well-founded doubt of the legitimacy of a Pope, to proceed to the election of another. [...]

[...] He hurled the most terrible anathemas against the Council and against the adherents of the other Pontiffs [...] **pretending that the Church of Jesus Christ, perished in all the other parts of the world, was to be found confined to Peñíscola alone**, as he answered the legates of the Council: *Ibi non est Ecclesia, sed in Paniscola est vera, inquam, Ecclesia, ... hic est arca Noe* [The Church is not there, but in Peñíscola, I say, is the true Church… here is Noah's ark]. **Whence it was possible, as Ballerini observes, to consider him a public schismatic and heretic, and consequently fallen *of himself* from the pontificate, even if he had been validly raised to it.** [...]

## § LII — Clement VIII: "a mere phantom of a pope" (pp. 89–90)

[...] Egidio Muñoz, canon of Barcelona, elected by only three cardinals under the name of Clement VIII, **universally derided by all in his vain pretensions, and therefore a mere phantom of a pope** [...] **recognized by all, without the least hesitation, as pontiff in name only** [...] a like indulgence was practised by the Council both with a pope who, like Gregory, was **at least doubtful**, and with one who, like Clement VIII, was **evidently a false pope**. [...]

## § LVI — Martin V a true legitimate pope (pp. 97–98)

[...] the Council did not exercise a sovereign power against the said pontiffs, since the first two gave up the papal dignity of themselves, and the other, whose election could be more reasonably suspected, was of himself subject to the Council, **as a doubtful pope, or even as already no longer pope: therefore Martin V could hold himself a true legitimate pope** without recognizing the vaunted conciliar supremacy [...]

## §§ XXXIV and LIX — the doubtful pope and the interregnum (pp. 56–57, 102)

[...] In the times of the antipopes, as also when a pope has died, the form of government ordained by Christ is not obscured; for both **in the case where there is a well-founded doubt, so that it is not well known who ought to be venerated as pope**, and in the case of a vacant see, there happens in the Church what happens in various monarchies, in which during an interregnum the government resides in some senate [...] But who does not know that this cannot be her natural state? Who can fail to recognize it from the very urgency with which the Church sets about electing her head, **ill enduring to remain headless for a long time?** [...]

What is this case? **That of a doubtful pope.** In this way the acts of the Council of Constance are reconciled with one another [...]
