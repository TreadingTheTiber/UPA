*Note on this entry: William of Ockham (c. 1287 – Munich, 1347), Oxford Franciscan and the greatest logician of his century, was summoned to Avignon in 1324 to answer for his Sentences commentary, was drawn there into the poverty controversy, and on 26 May 1328 fled the papal court with the Minister General Michael of Cesena, holding that John XXII had taught heresy in his constitutions on poverty and had thereby ceased to be pope. Excommunicated, he spent the rest of his life at the court of Louis of Bavaria writing the political works from which these blocks are taken: the vast* Dialogus *(Part I on heretics and the heretical pope, c. 1334–38), the* Compendium errorum papae Ioannis XXII*, and the* De dogmatibus Ioannis XXII*, his line-by-line prosecution of John's teaching of 1331–34 that the souls of the saints do not see God face to face until after the last judgment. Benedict XII defined the contrary in* Benedictus Deus *(1336); the campaign to have John judged a heretic and deposed went nowhere, and the tradition remembered Ockham on this question as the adversary. Witnesses here are two: Goldast's* Monarchia *(Hanau 1614), which prints the whole Dialogus in Roman type, for Books II and V; and a Lyons-family incunable of 1494 (Munich, 2 Inc.c.a. 511), gothic and contraction-dense, for the Compendium and the De dogmatibus, both transcribed at tier 1 from the page images since neither machine layer is legible. The British Academy critical edition (McGrade–Kilcullen) was used as control for the Dialogus.*

***Why this entry matters: the one witness to School 3b, and what he actually holds.*** *School 3b, as this collection defines it, is the position in which a minority's contradiction counts against a teaching, the general reception counts for nothing, and the Church at large is harmed. No author of the disputed question holds it; Ockham, a century and a half before the question was posed, does, and the texts show exactly how. He does* ***not*** *deny that the whole Church is protected. Book V opens (cap. 1, Goldast p. 467–468) by stating as common ground that "****the whole multitude of Christians cannot become heretical****" (*tota multitudo Christianorum haereticari non potest*), and the view he develops holds that "the universal college of Christians, which in fact comprehends men and women, God will never permit to err against the faith." What he denies is that any part of that whole shares the guarantee: "****what is promised to the whole and to no part must not be attributed to any part, even the more principal****" (lib. V cap. 22, Goldast p. 489), which he turns first against the Roman Church and, in the Compendium, against the pope. And he defines "the whole" as every member at once, so that the guarantee is satisfied by a single refuser: "if one alone dissented, such a truth would not be to be accepted, because* ***the whole faith of the Church can stand in one alone****, just as at the time of Christ's death the whole faith of the Church remained in the blessed Virgin alone" (lib. II cap. 27 of the critical edition, printed by Goldast as cap. XXV, p. 429; Goldast repeats* ecclesiae *where the critical text has* catholica*). Book V presses it to the limit, in arguments the Master sets out for the position that the multitude can err (cap. 34 carries his express disclaimer that what follows is not to be imputed to him): the tribulation "will perhaps be so great that* ***few of the faithful will remain firm in the faith****, and whether they will be men or women God alone knows" (cap. 34, Goldast p. 504–505; Goldast reads* pauci*, the critical text* paucissimi*); and, as the fifth argument of cap. 35 (p. 506), if "the whole multitude of Christians, men and women, having the use of reason" erred, "Christ's promises could be saved through baptized infants, for infants have the habit of faith." Put the two together and a single baptized infant suffices for the Church to be intact, and an infant cannot contradict anything: the guarantee does not even require a dissenting voice, only that not every member acquiesce. That is 3b in its extreme form, and it is why the acceptance of John XXII by the whole visible Church proved nothing to him.*

***The pope can err: Ockham's own voice.*** *The Dialogus is recitative; its Master sets out opinions, and the Sergius–Formosus passage of lib. II cap. 27 (Goldast cap. XXV, p. 428: "some hold the pope to be of such authority that he can condemn any assertion as heretical at his pleasure … and these are said to be imitators of Pope Sergius, the persecutor of Pope Formosus, who held that a pope cannot be damned") is recited with loaded framing, not asserted. The Compendium is not recitative. There, fol. 64v: "by the foregoing examples … it is evidently shown that* ***the pope can err against the faith and consequently be stained with heretical wickedness****"; the canon Si papa (D. 40) "openly supposes that the pope can deviate from the faith, and consequently become a heretic"; the gloss on A recta (C. 24 q. 1) understands the Church that cannot err "****not of the pope himself, who is called the church****"; "****it is certain that the pope can err****"; and "this is the case in which a pope can bind a pope." Fol. 65r draws the consequence: "****if a pope is a heretic, in that in which he is a heretic he is less than any catholic bishop****, because the Church notes the fact without a sentence"; mortals may judge the pope "at least in the crime of heresy"; "whoever can be deposed from the papacy has a superior by whom he is deposed, but a pope can be deposed on account of his crimes," with John XII deposed under Otto as precedent; and the four popes judged for faith — Anastasius, Marcellinus, Symmachus, and* ***Liberius "whom Eusebius the Roman presbyter declared a heretic"*** *(compare the [Liberius dossier](https://github.com/TreadingTheTiber/UPA/tree/main/0355%20Liberius%20Dossier)). Against Tierney's reading of Ockham as "an early champion of papal infallibility," Kilcullen's verdict stands on these texts: "Ockham was not a champion of papal infallibility. In fact he argues that no part of the Church is infallible." What Ockham does hold is that a definition once accepted by the whole Church is irreformable, which is the whole ground of his charge that John, in contradicting Nicholas III's* Exiit qui seminat*, taught heresy; but that irreformability flows from the acceptance, never from the office.*

***The machinery, and the opposite lesson drawn from it.*** *Lib. II cap. 1–2 (Goldast pp. 410–412) is the medieval source of the apparatus the affirmative tradition later uses. Beyond Scripture and what follows from it by necessary consequence, Ockham admits a class of catholic truths "which follow manifestly from the aforesaid truths together with certain other truths that consist in fact and cannot truly be denied … concerning which deeds and acts a rational Christian can sufficiently have faith": that "the four general councils rightly celebrated defined catholic truth," that "the faith Augustine held is to be reckoned catholic." A revealed universal, a reliably established fact, a singular that is of faith: this is the syllogism of every 1a author from Comitoli to Bartis, with "this man was legitimately elected" as the fact. Ockham built the instrument and drew the contrary conclusion, because for him the reliably established fact about a pope could be that he taught heresy, and the whole visible Church's acceptance could not overturn it.*

***The John XXII treatise (block E).*** *It opens on the Psalm, "the words of his mouth are iniquity and deceit," foreseeing "the counterfeit verbal protestation or revocation, the frivolous retraction, of John the Twenty-Second, containing superstitious heretical iniquity, indeed a deadly deceit … which he strives with fox-like cunning to cloak in ambiguous and deceitful words to lead the simple astray." Ockham then quotes John's own consistory of 3 January 1334, "founded on five ways," and dissects it "piece by piece, appending our own refutations": that whoever hopes for a future thing lacks the vision of God is heresy, since Christ hoped and yet saw the divine essence; that a soul seeing God cannot be further exalted is heresy, since Paul in rapture saw the divine essence and was afterward exalted; that the saints "murmuring" implore vengeance hints "a new manifest heresy, that the saints in heaven are not wholly freed from every evil." "That this is heretical is plainly clear"; John is "the inventor of these heresies," who "vainly boasts that he studied the originals of the saints."*

***Classification 3b.*** *Not School 1: the received pope can be no pope, and reception certifies no fact. Not School 2: "the pope can err against the faith," and his definitions are guaranteed only once the whole Church has accepted them. Not 3a: for Córdoba a definition not generally contradicted is safe and the general reception counts; for Ockham it counts for nothing against one dissenter. The evaluation belongs to whoever discerns the error, the majority is harmed, the remnant is the Church. He is filed at 1334, the year of the consistory he prosecutes and of the Dialogus's composition, and he is the reason the root README's statement that 3b has no witness now reads "one witness, before the question, rejected by every party to it."*

## Dialogus I lib. II cap. 27 (Goldast cap. XXV, p. 429) — the faith in one alone

**Discip.** What do they think, if all Christians except a few or one were to adhere to such an assertion, which could not be proved from the previous teaching of the Church? **Mag.** They say that **if one alone dissented, such a truth would not be to be accepted, because the whole faith of the Church can stand in one alone, just as at the time of Christ's death the whole faith of the Church remained in the blessed Virgin alone**; nor is it to be believed that at every time after the times of the Apostles there have been some more accepted by God than the Apostles were before Christ's death. [...]

## Dialogus I lib. V cap. 1, 22, 34, 35 — the whole, the part, the remnant, the infants (Goldast pp. 467–468, 489, 504–506)

**Cap. 1.** [...] since all Christians seem to hold that **the whole multitude of Christians cannot become heretical**, some however that neither can a general council, others that neither can the Roman Church, some that neither can the college of cardinals, and others that not even the pope can be polluted with heretical wickedness [...]

**Cap. 22.** **What is promised to the whole and to no part ought not to have been attributed to any part, even the more principal.** But never to err against the faith was promised by Christ to the whole congregation of the faithful, and to no part was this promised by Christ. Therefore this must not be attributed to any particular church of catholics. Since therefore the Roman Church is a part of the Church and is not the whole Church, not being able to err against the faith is not to be attributed to the Roman Church.

**Cap. 34.** [The Master, having said that what follows is not his own view:] [...] which tribulation will perhaps be so great that **few of the faithful will remain firm in the faith**; and whether those faithful will be men or women, God alone knows, and those to whom God has specially revealed it.

**Cap. 35.** [The fifth argument for the position that the multitude can err:] But with the whole multitude of Christians, men and women, having the use of reason erring, Christ's promises concerning the faith of Christ that is to remain can be saved. For Christ is truly spiritually with baptized infants through grace and the virtues. The faith of blessed Peter too, for which Christ prayed, would in no way fail, because it would be found in infants; **for infants have the habit of faith. Therefore, with the whole multitude of Christians having the use of reason erring, Christ's promises can be saved through baptized infants.** Therefore it is rash to assert that the whole multitude of Christians having the use of reason will never err against the faith.

## Compendium errorum, fols. 64v–65r — the pope can err; the heretic pope is less than any bishop

By the foregoing examples, and many others here omitted for brevity, it is evidently shown that **the pope can err against the faith and consequently be stained with heretical wickedness.** [...] "let no mortal presume to rebuke the faults of the pope of this holy see, for he who is to judge all is judged by no one, **unless he be found to have deviated from the faith**." Where it is openly supposed that the pope can deviate from the faith, and consequently become a heretic. Which the gloss on *A recta* openly asserts: that the church of which it is said that it cannot err is to be understood **not of the pope himself, who is called the church** [...] **it is certain that the pope can err** [...] this is the case in which a pope can bind a pope.

**Secunda obiectio.** [...] concerning the pope's deeds, at least in the crime of heresy, mortals do have the right to judge, as is plain from Pope Anastasius, from Pope Marcellinus, **from Pope Liberius whom Eusebius declared a heretic**, and from Pope Symmachus [...] From these and very many other cases it appears that a pope can be accused and judged, and consequently that, at least in the crime of heresy, he has a superior on earth [...] **If a pope is a heretic, in that in which he is a heretic he is less than any catholic bishop**, because the Church notes the fact without a sentence. [...] **Whoever can be deposed from the papacy has a superior by whom he is deposed; but a pope can be deposed on account of his crimes** [...]

## De dogmatibus Ioannis XXII, fol. 1r — the incipit

The words of his mouth are iniquity and deceit, says the psalmist, foreseeing in prophecy through the Spirit the counterfeit verbal protestation or revocation, that is, the frivolous retraction of John the Twenty-Second, **which contains superstitious heretical iniquity, indeed a deadly deceit**; and in that same protestation [...] he attempts to prove a manifest heresy by heretical arguments, and strives with fox-like cunning to cloak it with ambiguous and deceitful words in order to lead the simple astray. [...] "In the year of the Lord 1333, read out on Monday the third of January, the lord John the Twenty-Second held a public consistory [...] he himself, by word of mouth and with great fervor, strove to prove that the purified souls of the saints do not see God facially until after the day of judgment, briefly rehearsing his arguments, which are founded on five ways." [...] **But that this is heretical is plainly clear.**
