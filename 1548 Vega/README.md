*Note on this entry: Andrés de Vega (d. 1549), Franciscan, Master of Salamanca, was one of the theologians at Trent and its decree on justification is the subject of this book. He is entered here for a reason that has nothing to do with his subject:* ***he is the last unentered name on the negative side's roll-call***, *listed by Suárez ("*Ejus sequaces Cano, **Vega** et alii*") and by Ripalda ("*contra Turrecrematum, Cajetanum, Bannez, Canum, **Vegam**, Cordubam, Castrum*") among those who denied that it is of faith that this-in-number man is Pontiff — and cited at an exact locus,* libr. 9. in Tridentin. cap. 39, *by Francesco Albertini, Gravina, Gaspar Hurtado, Oviedo and Turrianus. Reading that chapter settles what he actually said, and the answer is not what either the roll-calls or a first glance suggest. Classification is* ***W***.*

***What the chapter is about.*** *Cap. XXXIX is "Ex certitudine caritatis nostræ in Deum, aut pœnitentiæ non potest colligi certitudo fidei de gratia" — whether one can reason one's way to certitude of being in a state of grace. The objection Vega must answer runs: it is of faith that whoever does what is in him obtains grace; I can be evidently certain that I do so; therefore I can be certain by faith that I am in grace. Vega refuses the two easy escapes. He will not deny the major (that would be "*absurdius aut magis impium*"), and* ***he will not deny the logic***. *He states the inference in the first person: "**But I myself could in no way be brought to this opinion; nor, however much I might wish it, could I doubt that it would be certain to me by faith that I am in grace, if indeed I could gather this from one premise believed and another evident to me**," and concludes, in the printed marginal's own words (*Certa est ex fide, conclusio illata ex una credita et altera euidenti*): "**Therefore that proposition which is inferred from one premise believed and another evident is by all to be reckoned, and to be believed, of faith.**" Whoever pertinaciously doubted such a conclusion "**would be a heretic**." His own escape is to deny the* ***minor***: *no one can know that he has really done what is in him, because unnoticed ignorances and errors creep in — and expressly* ***not*** *because our interior acts are hidden from us, a view he calls "absurd and openly heretical."*

***The papal sentence.*** *In the first of his four arguments for that logic — the argument that many propositions are held of faith though not expressed in Scripture, being deduced from what is contained there together with one naturally evident premise (Scotus's* Pater differt realiter a filio; *the Trinitarian and Eucharistic examples) — Vega writes, at* ***fol. 200r***:*

> *Idemque patet de propositionibus, quæ dependent a diffinitione Conciliorum generalium, uel Papę. Omnes enim illæ supponunt istas propositiones.* **Hoc Concilium est legitime congregatum. Hic Papa est rite, & canonice electus.** *Quæ quidem non sunt reuelatæ, sed euidenter cognitæ, atque ideo & postea diffinitæ, & ut certæ ex fide receptæ ab illis, qui non habuerunt de earum ueritate euidentiam.*

*"The same is clear of the propositions which depend on a definition of general councils, or of the Pope. For all of them presuppose these propositions:* ***This Council is legitimately convened. This Pope is rightly and canonically elected.*** *Which indeed are* ***not revealed, but evidently known*** *— and therefore afterwards also defined, and* ***received as certain of faith by those who did not have evidence of their truth***." *And a leaf earlier, among his counter-instances: "**Do not the Fathers in a general Council know by faith that it is a legitimate and general Council, before they define that proposition?**"*

***Why this matters, stated exactly.*** *Two claims are often made about Vega, and the text supports neither without qualification.* ***First***, *the roll-calls that put him among the deniers rest, so far as this chapter goes, on the half-sentence "*quæ quidem non sunt reuelatæ*" read apart from what surrounds it. Vega's whole point in the passage is the* opposite *of a denial: he is listing the papal and conciliar singulars as* ***examples of propositions rightly held of faith*** *by the very inference he is defending. Albertini, citing this exact locus, reports him correctly — his margin reads "**the truer opinion of Vega and of Cardinal Bellarmine**," and his text: "I say* ***with Vega lib. 9. in Trident. cap. 39.***, *and with Cardinal Bellarmine, and the more common opinion of the Scholastics,* ***that it is of faith***." *Note too how Suárez himself frames the school he assigns Vega to: "*aliqui ex Catholicis* ***negant posse immediate fide teneri***, *hunc esse verum Pontificem,* ***sed solum certitudine quadam morali***" — a denial of the* ***immediate*** *mode, not of the guarantee. The same correction has already been made for the other name in that margin: Báñez, Cano's own student, wrote in 1602 that "those are mistaken who say he absolutely affirms* non esse de fide hunc hominem…; *for without doubt he speaks* de fide immediate" *(the [1563 Cano](https://github.com/TreadingTheTiber/UPA/tree/main/1563%20Cano) and [1602 Báñez](https://github.com/TreadingTheTiber/UPA/tree/main/1602%20Banez) entries). Ripalda's list, for its part, includes Báñez himself.* ***A name in a roll-call is not a position.***

***Second — and this is why the classification is W and not higher*** — *Vega is nevertheless* ***not*** *a witness to the acceptance-doctrine, and the entry does not pretend otherwise. His guarantee runs through* ***evidentia***, *not through the Church's acceptance. He says the fact is* evidently known *(to those in a position to know it) and is afterwards defined and received on faith by the rest; he does* ***not*** *say that God prevents the whole Church from adhering to a false head, and* ***he nowhere poses that question***. *A full-text sweep of both his volumes bears this out: no* acceptatio, *no* receptus ab Ecclesia, *no doubtful pope, no schism (the word* schisma *occurs nowhere in the 1548 folio), no discussion of a pope teaching error. The 37 occurrences of* pontific- *are all incidental. What Vega supplies is the* ***apparatus*** *— the premise-rule and the taxonomy of certitude — with which the later dispute is conducted; the thesis is other men's. That is precisely what* ***W*** *names, and the classification key has been widened to cover this third ground alongside the patristic case and the divided-obedience case of Castro and Lensaeus.*

***The stakes he sets, without drawing them.*** *The historical value of the sentence is that it makes "*Hic Papa est rite, & canonice electus*"* ***the presupposed minor of every papal definition***. *That is the lever of the argument which, on Turrianus's account (the [1634 Turrianus](https://github.com/TreadingTheTiber/UPA/tree/main/1634%20Turrianus) entry), carried the affirmative side after Clement VIII: that on the contrary opinion a papal definition "would not have sufficient certitude, because it would not be certain that that definition proceeds from a true Pontiff." Vega states its premise in* ***1548***, *fifteen years before Cano's* De locis, *and does not draw the conclusion. Symmetrically, Suárez's own second argument* for *the negative — that the fact hangs on circumstances "*ut an sit **baptizatus**, an **rite ordinatus***" — is built out of Vega's catalogue at fol. 144v of the singulars knowable only by human faith.* ***Both sides of the later controversy are armed from this book.***

***The fallibility objection, and how far Vega goes.*** *Because it bears directly on the modern use of this material, the end of the argument should be given plainly, though it falls just past the transcribed block, on* ***fol. 201r*** *(read on the page image for this entry). Vega puts the sharpest form of the objection to himself (*Replica 2*): a proposition of faith is one under which falsehood can in no way lie; but where a proposition depends on something known only* by experience, *"falsehood can lie under it as under the experience," and one "cannot be certain that he cannot be deceived by it," since "he knows that many have often been deceived in similar assents,* ***even when they thought them evident***." *Vega's response is remarkable for its candour: "***Fortis quidem ac valida est hæc obiectio***" — "**this objection is indeed strong and valid**," and it may rightly cast some fear into anyone who would think himself altogether certain of his own grace. But he does not leave it standing. "**The more and more I think about this matter, the more unhesitatingly I judge that one who has evidence is not only certain that he is not deceived, but certain that he cannot be deceived in that to which he assents evidently. For through evidence it is certain that no one can be deceived**" — followed by the paper-in-the-hand argument: if I may be deceived in seeing this paper on which I write, even when I plainly see that I see it, by what shall I ever be certain? So the concession is aimed at* ***seeming*** *evidence in the introspective case, which is his own thesis about grace; genuine* evidentia *he holds to exclude deception. That distinction is what leaves the papal minor, which he classed as* euidenter cognita, *on the secure side of his own line — and it is also the distinction later authors had to argue about, since whether a canonical election is known* demonstratively *or only* by experience *is exactly the question Gravina would put ("must the natural premise be necessary and certain, so that no falsehood can underlie it, or does moral certitude suffice?"), listing Vega among those for whom moral certitude suffices.*

***The supporting blocks.*** *Cap. IV (fol. 144r–v) gives the* ***second grade of human faith***, *and its contents are the sacramental singulars: "that bishops, when they ordain ministers of the altar; priests, when they consecrate the most holy Eucharist, when they baptize, when they absolve… have the intention of doing what the Church intends." The ground is not evidence but the impossibility of living otherwise: "**And indeed the peace of consciences cannot stand, nor the sweetness and tranquillity of Christian life together, unless these things are held as certain and undoubted**" — with Innocent III's rule that of one born of Christian parents "**it is so strongly presumed that he was baptized, that this presumption is to be held for certitude**." Cap. XLI supplies the hard cases: "**it can be certain to no one, by faith or evidently… that he is truly Baptized, or absolved**," because the minister's intention is required and is not evident; and the case of one who receives orders "**from a bishop who is commonly reputed such**" when "**the bishop himself were not a bishop**" — in which event, if it later be established for certain, he must be ordained again, and "**he would neither have consecrated the Eucharist at that time, nor absolved anyone**." Cap. XLVI states the settled practice: "**We priests also all believe, without any hesitation, that we were ordained by the bishops who outwardly ordained us**," though the intention "is not evident to us, nor established by divine revelation." This cluster is the material later authors actually transfer to the papal case, and it is where Vega is most original.*

***Block A*** *is included for a different reason. Writing as a participant — "***Oculatus testis hæc loquor***," I speak these things as an eyewitness — Vega explains why Trent did not repeat Constance's title* universalem ecclesiam repraesentans: *at Constance it was "most convenient to employ that title" in order to end "that great schism," so that those "who bore themselves as supreme pontiffs" might yield; "**But now, since the Church of God rejoices in one — and that an undoubted — supreme Pontiff, there was no cause to compel the use of such a title.**" The distinction between a Church with one undoubted head and a Church with rival claimants is drawn here as a matter of course, by a theologian on the floor of the council, three years before the controversy this collection documents had a name.*

## Lib. IX, cap. XXXIX — the papal and conciliar singulars (fol. 200r)

The same is clear of the propositions which depend on a definition of general Councils, or of the Pope. For all of them presuppose these propositions: **This Council is legitimately convened. This Pope is rightly and canonically elected.** Which indeed are **not revealed, but evidently known** — and therefore afterwards also defined, and **received as certain of faith by those who did not have evidence of their truth.**

## Lib. IX, cap. XXXIX — the Fathers know by faith that their Council is legitimate (fol. 199v)

**Do not the Fathers in a general Council know by faith that it is a legitimate and general Council, before they define that proposition?**

## Lib. IX, cap. XXXIX — the thesis, refused to be surrendered (fol. 199v, 200v)

**But I myself could in no way be brought to this opinion; nor, however much I might wish it, could I doubt that it would be certain to me by faith that I am in grace, if indeed I could gather this from one premise believed and another evident to me.**

[...] **Therefore that proposition which is inferred from one premise believed and another evident is by all to be reckoned, and to be believed, of faith. Nor ought the argument made to the contrary move anyone.** [...] **And so on this side nothing will be wanting to it, to prevent its being reckoned to be of faith.**

## Lib. IX, cap. IV — the second grade of human faith (fol. 144v)

Such are: that bishops, when they ordain ministers of the altar; priests, when they consecrate the most holy Eucharist, when they baptize, when they absolve, or administer any other sacrament, **have the intention of ordaining, baptizing, consecrating, absolving, and of doing what the Church intends** [...] **And indeed the peace of consciences cannot stand, nor the sweetness and tranquillity of Christian life together, unless these things are held as certain and undoubted.** Whence most prudently Innocent III [...] writes: of him who was born of Christian parents and has been faithfully conversant among Christians, **it is so strongly presumed that he was baptized, that this presumption is to be held for certitude**, until perhaps the contrary should be proved by the most evident arguments.

## Lib. IX, cap. XLI — the bishop who was not a bishop (fol. 211r)

**However much a baptized man might believe, and will, that he receives orders from a bishop who is commonly reputed such, if nevertheless by some chance or by the malice of the bishop or of another — or the bishop himself were not a bishop, or had not had any intention of ordaining him — and this should afterwards be established for certain, without doubt he would have to be ordained again, that he might be a priest**; and, as the doctors of theology commonly hand down, whatever some of the canonists may have thought, **neither would he have consecrated the Eucharist at that time, nor absolved anyone**, inasmuch as he would have had no power truly fit for performing these things.

## Lib. IX, cap. XLVI — what priests believe without hesitation (fol. 216r)

**We priests also all believe, without any hesitation, that we were ordained by the bishops who outwardly ordained us. And with the same certitude we believe that priests truly consecrate the Eucharist, as often as we assist at the sacrifice of the altar — although neither evidently, nor by divine revelation, is the intention established to us, whether of the ordaining bishop or of the baptizing or consecrating priest, which we know for certain by faith to be necessary for the confecting of these sacraments.** Nor is there anyone who would brand any man with the note of temerity or rashness on this account; nay, all judge it culpable **to hesitate** even the least about any of these things, save for the gravest causes.

## Lib. I, cap. V — one, and that an undoubted, supreme Pontiff (fol. 5r)

Nor was that title, which the Council of Constance often uses — namely, *representing the universal Church* — omitted for the reasons which certain men malignantly allege [...] For at that time, in order to compose that great schism which then prevailed, it was most convenient to employ that title, so that they who bore themselves as supreme pontiffs, while acknowledging themselves inferior to the universal Church, might yield their pontifical authority, at the command of the Council which represented her, for the peace and tranquillity of the Church. **But now, since the Church of God rejoices in one — and that an undoubted — supreme Pontiff, there was no cause to compel the use of such a title.** [...] **I speak these things as an eyewitness.**
