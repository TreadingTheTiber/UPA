But this is not to untie the argument, but to render it stronger and more perplexed, and, so to speak, more knotty. Was not faith in the prophets, before the approbation of the Church, extended to assenting to many contingent facts revealed to them by God? Can not anyone also by faith assent, and is he not bound to assent, that he is in grace, where God should reveal this to him? Which they proved also by this reasoning. The certitude of faith is, according to St Thomas and Scotus and others, greater than the certitude of evidence; and that not only, as a certain learned man interprets Thomas, in itself, but also as regards us and on the part of our intellect and affection. For we assent more certainly and more firmly to things revealed by God than to things experienced and demonstrated. Which indeed proceeds partly from the greater propensity of our affection, partly from the fuller help of God and the concurrence of infused faith. Since therefore a conclusion cannot be more certain than the premises from which it is inferred, a conclusion drawn from one premise believed and another evident will not be certain with the certitude of faith. And to this perhaps John Baconthorpe looked, when he said that that proposition, *I am in grace*, is to no one *de fide*, but can be certain with a certitude consequent upon faith.
