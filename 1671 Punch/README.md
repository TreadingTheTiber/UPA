*Note on this entry: John Punch (Ioannes Poncius, c. 1603–1661), the Irish Franciscan of St. Isidore's, Rome, and Paris, was the great systematizer of Scotism; this quaestio of his posthumously printed* Cursus *(Lyon 1671) was composed under Innocent X, whom it names as reigning. It earns its place in the collection twice over. First, as* ***proposal-theory***: the quaestio is titled "Who has the authority of proposing the things of faith," and runs as one continuous argument through ten conclusions — the ordinary proposers (bishops, pastors, professors), the need for an infallible rule, Scripture, Scripture's insufficiency alone, the private spirit rejected, unwritten tradition, particular councils excluded, general councils, and finally the pope — so that the UPA doctrine arrives as the capstone of a treatise on* proposal *(compare the 1669 Ferré entry and the proposal-side census). Second, for the doctrine itself, at Conclusio X: against the objection that nothing the pope proposes can be of infallible authority unless "it were de fide that Innocent X, who reigns today, was canonically and truly elected," Punch answers (n. 14, with the marginal note "He who is commonly held for Pope without sufficient reason for doubting is to be believed de fide to be the true Pope"): "***truly I judge it to be an article of faith that whoever is commonly held by the Church as canonically and legitimately elected, without reasonable cause of doubting, is the true Pope***, especially after he should actually exercise his authority." His third proof is the collection's most explicit* ***reception-as-Apostolic-tradition*** *argument: "from time to time, from the beginning of the Church until now… the faithful believed that those who were held, without reason for doubting, for Pontiffs, were truly such — in which there certainly would not be such general agreement,* ***unless there were an Apostolic tradition on that matter. And so without doubt Christ revealed this truth to the Apostles, and they to others.***" Before that main thesis he lodges a remarkable fallback (n. 13): moral certitude would suffice for divine faith in the definitions, and the tradition of papal infallibility probably "extends itself to all Popes who are known with moral evidence to be such…* ***whether they truly be such or not***" — the effect-guarantee hedged under the fact-guarantee, the same two-pronged architecture as the 1632 Turnbull entry. The completeness clause is stated (a doubt is "reasonable" when the electors split between claimants or manifest force is applied), the woman/unbaptized cases are absorbed by providence ("God would never permit that they should proceed to the decision of any doctrine of faith" — indeed providence prevents such elections or exposes them "immediately, in the very beginning"), and n. 15 adds a juridical* ***sanatio*** *doctrine found almost nowhere else in the collection: hidden simony or coercion "***do not impede the validity of the election***" when the defects are not publicly established and no principal party opposes — "by a valid election we understand that election which, being posited, the one elected is truly Pope" — with the more probable alternative that providence simply keeps such defects out of any election "made peacefully, and without tumult," or removes them "through retractation, or penance, or some other way." (This is Pallavicino's second-election/sanatio complex, argued independently by a Scotist.) Nn. 16–17 close the quaestio on whether pope or council can make a new article of faith (no new revelation; canonization treated at length, anticipating the Verricelli 1653 entry's q. 24). Punch appears in Diana's affirmative census as "Poncius" (reported in the repo's 1684 Murga entry, n. 79). The census citation should read* ***disp. 28, q. 1, concl. 10, nn. 13–14*** *(the conciliar-rule conclusio is concl. 9, n. 12). Classification is* ***1a*** *("articulum fidei"), with the n. 13 effect-guarantee hedge noted.*

## Disputatio XXVIII, Quaestio I — *Quis habet authoritatem proponendi res fidei*

**CONCLUSIO X.** **13.** *The Roman Pontiff, who is the Supreme Pontiff of the whole Church, has authority of this kind even outside a Council.* [...]

*It is objected secondly*: In order that what was proposed by the Pope should be of infallible authority, it would have to be believed that he himself is the true Pope; and thus it would be de fide that **Innocent X., who reigns today, was canonically and truly elected**: but this is not true [...]

*I respond first* by denying the consequence of the major: because **the moral certitude which would be had that he is the true Pope suffices** for this, that someone may believe by divine faith the things proposed by him; and certainly I judge it sufficiently probable that **the Apostolic tradition which is had concerning the infallible authority of the Pope extends itself to all Popes who are known with moral evidence to be such, whether they be believed by divine faith to be such or not, and also whether they truly be such or not** [...]

**14.** *I respond secondly* by denying the minor: for **truly I judge it to be an article of faith that whoever is commonly held by the Church as canonically and legitimately elected, without reasonable cause of doubting, is the true Pope — especially after he should actually exercise his authority** as to deciding some controversies of faith, or the convocation of a general Council.

*This is proved*: because it is de fide that God gave to Peter and to his successors infallible authority of this kind; but **if it were not believed in particular that to this and to that successor such authority has been granted [...] it would serve for nothing, and such authority would be communicated in vain to the successors of Peter, because it could always be doubted who was his true successor** [...]

*It is proved thirdly*: Because **from time to time, from the beginning of the Church until now, as is established from the Ecclesiastical Histories, the faithful believed that those who were held, without reason for doubting, for Pontiffs, were truly such — in which there certainly would not be such general agreement, unless there were an Apostolic tradition on that matter. And so without doubt Christ revealed this truth to the Apostles, and they to others.** [...]

**15.** [...] *But in this sense* I judge that it does not follow that the election is good; because [...] **it must altogether be said that these defects [simony, coercion] do not impede the validity of the election**, although they would impede the validity of other elections, and of this one also, if they became publicly known [...] *It could, nevertheless,* be said with sufficient probability [...] that **the election will always be without these defects when the contrary is not publicly established, and no opposition is made on that account; because this seems to pertain to the particular providence of God** [...]
