*Note on this entry: Pedro Ojea, S.J., who had taught theology in the Jesuit college at Zaragoza, published this treatise on faith in 1661 under the Aragon provincial's licence. His "celebrated corollary" names the reigning pope,* ***Alexander VII***, *"who now presides over the Church as these things are written," and is a clean and well-argued* ***1a***: *divine faith of the singular, on the express condition of the Church's acceptance, with the exercise-condition nowhere required. Its value to the collection is threefold: it states the acceptance mechanism with a precision found in few other entries; it answers, seventeen years in advance and point by point, the argument [Neesen](https://github.com/TreadingTheTiber/UPA/tree/main/1678%20Neesen) would make in 1678; and it carries a first-hand narrative of the* ***Alcalá affair*** *that supplies the disposition of the case.*

***The threshold — the peaceful case only.*** *§ 47 restricts the question before arguing it. If the matter is in litigation, with "sufficient reason for prudently doubting whether he was rightly and canonically elected, and whether another has a better right to be held for Pope, many learned men of good conscience plausibly defending either side," then "**although he really is the true Pope, yet on account of the insufficiency of the proposal it will not yet be possible to believe by faith that he is such.**" And then the sentence that governs everything after it: "***Quaestio ergo procedit de illo, quem Universa Ecclesia agnoscit, ac recipit ut verum Papam***" — the question proceeds concerning him* ***whom the Universal Church acknowledges and receives as true Pope***. *The completeness clause is here a clause about* ***proposal***: *a contested claimant is excluded not because he is not pope but because the Church has not yet sufficiently proposed him.*

***The four negative arguments he sets himself.*** *§§ 49–52 give the other side its full strength: (1) from act to potency — to be of faith that this man is Pope it must be of faith that he is a subject proximately capable, i.e. validly baptized, which is not of faith; (2) the papacy is conferred by Christ upon canonical election, and "that this man, namely Alexander VII, was canonically elected, is nowhere found revealed by God"; (3) the* ***self-proposal objection*** *— the revelation "is proposed to us by no one except by him who says that he is the true Pope, by the very fact that he bears himself as such;* ***which indeed is not sufficient***"; *(4) that it need not be of faith for his definitions to be believed, since it is not of faith that our parents, preachers or parish priests teach us rightly. The third is Albertini's mechanism raised against him; the fourth is precisely Neesen's reply to the definition-certitude argument, conceded here in advance.*

***The assertion, and the periodization.*** *§ 53: "***Nevertheless I assert that it is of faith that Alexander VII is the true Pope.*** *This is now the common opinion, from which,* ***since Clement VIII*** *— who on the occasion I related above opposed himself to the contrary opinion —* ***no one now dissents***." *That is Turrianus's periodization, stated independently twenty-seven years later; and Neesen, seventeen years later still, is its counter-instance. The first foundation is Martin V's Constance bull — returning heretics must believe "the canonically elected Pope, whoever he shall be for the time being (***his name being expressed***), to be the successor of Peter" — which "could not be believed if this were not an object immediately and proximately credible by faith."*

***The mechanism — acceptance as a second election.*** *§ 54 states the minor of the universal-contains-singular syllogism in the acceptance-idiom outright: "since it is evidently established that Alexander VII was elected, or assumed to the Roman Chair,* ***through the acceptance of the Church, which is the legitimate election***, *and without any voiding impediment." § 55 objects that many impediments of ecclesiastical law void a conclave, and we have no evidence, physical or moral, that none intervened. § 56 is the entry's centre:*

> *impedimenta iure Pontificio inducta, quae reddunt irritam electionem Pontificis,* ***solum respicere electionem illam, quam faciunt Cardinales in Conclavi.*** *Per hanc vero electionem non redditur fide certum, quod hic sit verus Papa:* ***post eam vero electionem sequitur altera, quae fit ab Ecclesia, acceptante eam personam in Summum Pontificem, ad quam electionem non se extendunt impedimenta iure Pontificio electionem irritantia.***

*"The impediments introduced by Pontifical law which void a Pontiff's election* ***regard only that election which the Cardinals make in Conclave***. *By this election it is not rendered certain by faith that this man is the true Pope: but* ***after that election there follows another, which is made by the Church, accepting that person as Supreme Pontiff — to which election the impediments of Pontifical law that void an election do not extend***." *He then gives the two-tier rule in full: if some such impediment "had crept into the election made by the Cardinals alone,* ***through this later election that defect would be supplied***, *provided it were not of divine law — namely not being a man but a woman, not being a Christian —* ***which impediments God will never permit to happen in this second election***." *Defects of ecclesiastical law the Church's acceptance* ***supplies***; *defects of divine law God* ***prevents***. *It is the Barbarius rule of the [1639 Bonacina](https://github.com/TreadingTheTiber/UPA/tree/main/1639%20Bonacina) entry and the providence-prevents argument of [Pueroni](https://github.com/TreadingTheTiber/UPA/tree/main/1633%20Pueroni), attached explicitly to the acceptance conceived as a second* electio, *with the certifying proposition set in his own italics: "***Iam Ecclesia hanc personam acceptavit in Summum Pontificem***." § 59 draws the consequence for the baptism objection: "by the very fact that it is of faith that Alexander VII is the true Pope, it is of faith that he is truly and validly baptized; for* ***if he were not baptized, God could not have permitted him to be rightly and canonically elected***."*

***What the acceptance does to the negative side's analogies and objections.*** *§ 57 breaks the host and contrition parallels on evidence: God revealed that every rightly consecrated host contains Christ, "but we do not have moral evidence that* this *host has been rightly consecrated," nor that anyone's contrition was such as it ought to be — whereas of the acceptance we do. § 61 answers self-proposal: the revelation "is proposed to the faithful through the evidence had of this, that Alexander VII is* ***canonically elected, and accepted by the Church***; *for by that very fact there is had an evidence* ***excluding all prudent and reasonable fear*** *that Alexander VII is a singular of the universal revealed by God." And § 62, remarkably, concedes the fourth argument outright: even if it were not of faith that this person is Pope, what he defines would still be of faith and obligatory — "***Which indeed they themselves admit who oppose us in this matter***" — so the ground of the thesis is not the certitude of definitions but the foundations already given. Neesen's whole escape from the definition-certitude argument is thus granted by an affirmative author before Neesen made it, and shown not to touch the question.*

***Sectio VI — the rule the whole thing rests on, and the point of division with Neesen.*** *§ 41: "that singular which is established by* ***moral evidence*** *to be contained under a revealed universal is likewise to be reckoned revealed, and proximately credible by divine faith, when no error in fact underlies the moral evidence" — citing Pedro Hurtado, Lugo "with Suárez, Bellarmine and others," Ripalda, Oviedo and Amico. The demand that this evidence "must be metaphysical or physical, and that moral does not suffice,* ***libere dicitur***" — is said gratuitously — "since this evidence is required for nothing but proposing the revelation to the intellect, and it is agreed among all that the knowledge by which the revelation is proposed need not be metaphysically or physically certain … as is clear in the proposal made by a parish priest." Neesen's Q. I dub. VI takes exactly the opposite side — a conclusion from a de fide premise and a merely naturally-known premise "cannot be believed by faith" — and his Dub. VIII is built on it. Ojea also meets the objection that one should rather have recourse to the Church's proposal: "the proposal of the Church, as governed by the Holy Spirit,* ***is not mere proposal but revelation***," *and besides it the proposal of parish priests and catechists is in any case required, "which has no greater certitude than that of which we here speak."*

***The Alcalá affair — the disposition of the case.*** *§ 48 lists the negative side — "Turrecremata, Cajetan, Cano, Vega, Córdoba, Castro and others, whom Gaspar Hurtado reports at disp. 11 diff. 13" — and then narrates, from Hurtado's own testimony, what the [1601 Alcalá Theses](https://github.com/TreadingTheTiber/UPA/tree/main/1601%20Alcala%20Theses) dossier documents only up to the 1602 Roman censure:*

> *ibidemque testatur* ***se ipsum eandem defendisse apud Complutensem Academiam in Actu, qui dicitur Magna, cum esset Collega Collegii Maioris.*** *Additque* ***rem fuisse delatam ad Pontificem, qui tunc erat Clemens VIII, qui iussit tam ipsum, quam Doctores, qui thesibus subscripserant, adire Romam*** *ad rationem dictae propositionis reddendam.* ***Postea vero concessit ut res in Hispania transigeretur per Iudices quos ad hoc designavit, qui cum rem exacte discussissent, tam ipsum Hurtadum, quam Doctores, qui thesibus subscripserant, innoxios et indemnes, doctrinamque sanam declararunt.***

*Hurtado "testifies that he himself defended the same [opinion] at the Complutensian Academy, in the Act which is called* Magna, *when he was a Fellow of the Colegio Mayor. He adds that the matter was referred to the Pontiff, who was then Clement VIII, who ordered both him and the Doctors who had subscribed the theses to go to Rome to render an account of the said proposition. But afterwards he granted that the matter be settled in Spain by Judges whom he designated for this purpose, who, when they had examined the matter exactly,* ***declared both Hurtado himself and the Doctors who had subscribed the theses innocent and unharmed, and the doctrine sound***." *This corroborates and sharpens the [1632 Hurtado](https://github.com/TreadingTheTiber/UPA/tree/main/1632%20Hurtado%20%28Gaspar%29) entry's "judged completely sound," and supplies the route: a Roman summons, then remission to Spanish judges, then acquittal. § 53 adds the sequel our Hurtado entry had left open — the affirmative "which Fr. Gaspar Hurtado also afterwards held, disp. 11 diff. 13,* ***retractata ea quam voce defenderat in Complutensi Academia***" *— having retracted the one he had defended orally at Alcalá. § 48 closes: "The same [negative] opinion is held by Vázquez,* ***Torres***, *and Bellarmine, according to Oviedo, contr. 4 pun. 7." That Torres is [Luis de Torres](https://github.com/TreadingTheTiber/UPA/tree/main/1634%20Turrianus), who presided over the Jesuit re-defence of the thesis at Alcalá in 1602 and, like Hurtado, later held the affirmative; the identification and its evidence are set out in that entry.*

***Ojea and Neesen, seventeen years apart.*** *Neesen's Dub. VIII of 1678 argues that the singular fails at the minor because canonical election rests on four conditions of divine law we cannot know — man, baptized, ordained, elected by legitimate electors. Ojea in 1661 states that objection (§§ 49–50, § 55), and answers it: the conclave election does not make the fact certain by faith; the Church's acceptance is a second election which supplies ecclesiastical defects and in which God prevents divine-law ones. Neesen's Q. I dub. VI holds that moral certitude of the minor cannot yield a conclusion of faith; Ojea's § 41 calls that demand gratuitous. Neesen's escape from the definition-certitude argument — that the proposed can be of faith though the proposer is known only by human faith — Ojea concedes in § 62 and shows to be beside the point. A full-text sweep of Neesen's 384 pages finds no citation of Ojea, Oviedo, Hurtado, Suárez or Turrianus, and no reference to Alcalá: the two men are not in conversation, and the affirmative side's answers to Neesen's arguments were already in print when he wrote. Classification is* ***1a***.*

## Sectio VII, § 47 — the threshold: the peaceful case only (printed 177)

**47** The difficulty proceeds concerning **a Pope existing in peaceful possession**; for if the matter be in litigation, and there be sufficient reason for prudently doubting whether he was rightly and canonically elected, and whether another has a better right to be held for Pope, many learned men of good conscience plausibly defending either side — **although he really is the true Pope, yet, on account of the insufficiency of the proposal, it will not yet be possible to believe by faith that he is such.** The question therefore proceeds concerning him **whom the Universal Church acknowledges and receives as true Pope.**

## Sectio VII, § 48 — the negative side, and the Alcalá affair (printed 177)

**48** The negative side is held by Torquemada, Cajetan, Cano, Vega, Córdoba, Castro, and others, whom Gaspar Hurtado reports at *disp. 11. diff. 13.*, and there testifies that **he himself defended the same at the Complutensian Academy, in the Act which is called *Magna*, when he was a Fellow of the Colegio Mayor**. He adds that **the matter was referred to the Pontiff, who was then Clement VIII, who ordered both him and the Doctors who had subscribed the theses to go to Rome to render an account of the said proposition**. But afterwards he granted that the matter be settled in Spain by Judges whom he designated for this purpose, who, when they had examined the matter exactly, **declared both Hurtado himself and the Doctors who had subscribed the theses innocent and unharmed, and the doctrine sound**. The same opinion is held by Vázquez, Torres, and Bellarmine, according to Oviedo, *contr. 4. pun. 7.*

## Sectio VII, § 53 — the assertion, and "no one now dissents" (printed 179)

**53** **Nevertheless I assert that it is of faith that Alexander VII is the true Pope.** This is now the common opinion, from which, **since Clement VIII** — who, on the occasion which I related above, and in the manner I there described, opposed himself to the contrary opinion — **no one now dissents**. The authors defending this opinion may be seen in Oviedo, *contr. 4. pun. 7.*; which Fr. Gaspar Hurtado also afterwards held, *disp. 11. diff. 13.*, **having retracted the one which he had defended orally at the Complutensian Academy**. The first foundation is drawn from the Bull of Martin V in the Council of Constance, in which the Pontiff says that Heretics returning to the Church are to believe that the canonically elected Pope, whoever he shall be for the time being (**his name being expressed**), is the successor of Peter. But if this were not an object immediately and proximately credible by faith, they could not believe it.

## Sectio VII, § 54 — acceptance is the legitimate election (printed 180)

[...] It is therefore revealed by God that everyone who has been elected by legitimate and canonical election, or assumed to the Roman Chair, is the successor of Peter, the Vicar of Christ, the head of the Church, and in a word the true Pope: but since it is evidently established that Alexander VII was elected, or assumed to the Roman Chair, **through the acceptance of the Church, which is the legitimate election**, and without any voiding impediment: therefore it is revealed by God that Alexander VII is the true Pope.

## Sectio VII, § 56 — the second election, which the impediments do not reach (printed 180)

**56** I answer that the impediments introduced by Pontifical law, which void a Pontiff's election, **regard only that election which the Cardinals make in Conclave. By this election, however, it is not rendered certain by faith that this man is the true Pope: but after that election there follows another, which is made by the Church, accepting that person as Supreme Pontiff — to which election the impediments of Pontifical law that void an election do not extend.** And accordingly, even though some impediment and defect of that kind had crept into the election made by the Cardinals alone, **through this later election that defect would be supplied** — provided it were not of divine law, namely not being a man but a woman, not being a Christian, **which impediments God will never permit to happen in this second election**. Through this second election, therefore — by which, namely, it is true to say, ***Now the Church has accepted this person as Supreme Pontiff*** — it is rendered certain by faith that that person is the true Pontiff.

## Sectio VII, § 59 — providence prevents (printed 181)

**59** [...] for by the very fact that it is of faith that Alexander VII is the true Pope, by that very fact it is of faith that he is truly and validly baptized; for **if he were not baptized, God could not have permitted him to be rightly and canonically elected**. For after He revealed that he who is rightly and canonically elected is the true Pope, He cannot permit that an incapable person, such as one unbaptized, be rightly and canonically elected.

## Sectio VII, §§ 61–62 — self-proposal answered; the definition argument conceded and set aside (printed 181–182)

**61** [...] For this revelation is proposed to the faithful through the evidence which is had of this, that Alexander VII is **canonically elected, and accepted by the Church**; for by that very fact there is had an evidence **excluding all prudent and reasonable fear** that Alexander VII is a singular of the universal revealed by God.

**62** [...] for even if it were not of faith that such a person is the true Pope, the things which he defined would be of faith, and there would be an obligation of believing them as of faith. **Which indeed they themselves admit who oppose us in this matter.** [...] The reason, therefore, why it is of faith that Alexander VII is the true Pope is not that which is assigned in that fourth argument, but those which we have brought to prove our Conclusion.

## Sectio VI, § 41 — moral evidence suffices; the strict demand is gratuitous (printed 174)

**41** Nevertheless I assert, with the common opinion of the more recent authors, that **that singular which is established by moral evidence to be contained under a revealed universal is likewise to be reckoned revealed, and proximately credible by divine faith, when no error in fact underlies the moral evidence.** [...] But that this evidence must be metaphysical or physical, and that moral evidence does not suffice, **is said gratuitously**. For since this evidence is required for nothing but proposing the revelation to the intellect, and it is agreed among all that the knowledge by which the revelation is proposed to the intellect need not be metaphysically or physically certain, but suffices to be morally certain — indeed not even so much is required, as is clear in the proposal of a parish priest — it is unjustly denied that this moral evidence, of the fact that this singular is contained in the revealed universal, suffices for such a singular to be reckoned comprehended under that revelation. Nor does it avail to have recourse to the proposal of the Church. For the proposal of the Church, as governed by the Holy Spirit, **is not mere proposal, but revelation**, as I shall say presently; and besides it there is altogether required the proposal of parish priests and catechists, which has no greater certitude than that of which we here speak.
